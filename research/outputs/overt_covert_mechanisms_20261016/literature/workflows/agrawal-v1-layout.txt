C. Choice of Time Horizon
We tested various time horizons, i.e., the number of rounds, and found that trading sessions of 30 rounds were enough to
observe a wide range of agent behavior. Sessions with 20 rounds were sometimes insufficient for bid-ask convergence, while
sessions with over 50 rounds grew stagnant after convergence was achieved.

E. Reliability of LLM Judgments
E.1. Motivation
In our setup, we employ an LLM-as-a-judge to score the level of seller coordination based on their reasoning traces. These
scores are assigned on an ordinal scale from 1 to 4 and influence both our per-round coordination metrics and overall
conclusions about agent behavior. Prior work has demonstrated the potential and limitations of using LLMs as evaluators
(Zheng et al., 2023; Bedemariam et al., 2025; Thakur et al., 2025), and emphasizes the importance of measuring not just
validity but reliability—the consistency of judgments across repeated evaluations.
Following best practices in recent literature (Schroeder & Wood-Doughty, 2024; Yamauchi et al., 2025), we treat the LLM as
a stochastic evaluator and quantify its reliability using two complementary classes of metrics: internal-consistency reliability
and inter-replica agreement.

E.2. Methodology
To assess reliability, we randomly sampled 500 uncorrelated rounds from our full corpus of 11,250 simulation rounds and
obtained 10 independent judgments for each using GPT-4.1-mini, the same evaluator model employed throughout our
main experiments. All judgments were generated at a temperature of 0.1 (as in the main experiments), using a different
random seed for each of the 10 replications per round. The ordinal scores from the judgments were then compiled into a
500 × 10 matrix.

(A) Internal-Consistency Reliability We compute McDonald’s Omega (ω) as our primary internal consistency measure.
Omega reflects the proportion of variance in the aggregate score that can be attributed to a common latent factor—in this
case, the LLM’s scoring rubric. Compared to Cronbach’s Alpha, Omega handles non-tau-equivalent loadings and provides a
more accurate estimate in factor-analytic settings (McNeish, 2018).
We compute ω using the reliabiliPy Python package (Fernández, 2022), which estimates polychoric correlations and
fits a single-factor model to yield both ωtotal and Cronbach’s α.

(B) Inter-Rater Agreement To complement internal-consistency metrics, we calculate Krippendorff’s Alpha (α) with
ordinal distance weighting. This measure evaluates agreement among the 10 replica raters, correcting for chance and
accounting for the ordinal scale. Unlike Cohen’s or Fleiss’ κ, Krippendorff’s α generalizes to multiple raters and gracefully
handles missing values, making it especially suitable for LLM-generated outputs. We computed this measure using the Fast
Krippendorff Python library (Castro, 2017).

E.3. Results
  • McDonald’s Omega (ωtotal ): 0.996
  • Cronbach’s Alpha (α): 0.995

  • Krippendorff’s Alpha (ordinal): 0.948

These values indicate extremely high reliability. The ω and α scores confirm that our LLM judge applies a stable rubric
across replications, while Krippendorff’s α suggests that seed-to-seed agreement remains well above chance, even on a
moderately fine-grained ordinal scale.
Our findings indicate that the judge model behaves reliably and deterministically under our prompt and temperature setting.
This supports our use of an LLM as a judge for measuring seller coordination. Moreover, it demonstrates that meaningful
judgment reliability can be achieved without human raters, provided the LLM prompt is well-calibrated.
